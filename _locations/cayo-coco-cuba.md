# Cayo Coco / Cayo Guillermo, Cuba (CCC) — Vacation Guide for Couples from Toronto

**Trip profile:** Two adults departing Toronto Pearson (YYZ), late April 2026, approximately 5 nights.
**Currency note:** Cuba uses the Cuban Peso (CUP) as its sole official currency. For tourists, **Canadian dollars (CAD) and Euros (EUR)** are the most practical foreign currencies to bring — they are readily exchanged at CADECA offices and resort front desks. Avoid bringing USD: the US dollar incurs a 10% exchange penalty in Cuba. Credit cards issued by Canadian banks generally work at some ATMs and resort cash desks, but reliability is inconsistent — do not rely on cards exclusively. Most visitors to Cayo Coco are on all-inclusive packages where cash use is minimal.

## Hotels

Cayo Coco and Cayo Guillermo operate almost entirely on an **all-inclusive model**. Unlike George Town or Nassau, there is essentially no independent hotel or guesthouse infrastructure accessible to tourists on these cays — you are booking a resort, and the resort is your world for the duration of the stay. This is not a criticism: the beaches and waters here are exceptional, and the all-inclusive model suits the destination well.

The two cays sit within the **Jardines del Rey (Gardens of the King) archipelago** on Cuba's north coast. Cayo Guillermo, the smaller of the two, is widely regarded as having the better beach (Playa Pilar). Cayo Coco is larger, has more resorts, more infrastructure, and a wider range of hotels. They are connected to each other and to mainland Cuba by long causeways (pedraplenes) — no boat required.

**Why choose Cayo Coco over Varadero?** Cayo Coco is less developed and feels more remote. The beaches are less crowded, the waters are noticeably clearer (less boat traffic, less development), the flamingo population is a unique attraction, and the overall atmosphere is quieter and more intimate. The trade-off is less variety — fewer resorts, virtually no life outside the resorts, and limited dining options beyond your hotel.

## Dining

Cayo Coco and Cayo Guillermo are almost entirely all-inclusive territory. Your resort provides all meals and drinks; the culinary experience is defined almost entirely by your choice of hotel.

**Cuban resort dining context:** Cuban all-inclusive dining has improved substantially over the past decade, driven by competition and international brand management (Meliá, Iberostar, Accor). The main buffets are large and well-stocked; all 4-5 star properties now include à la carte specialty restaurant dinners — typically Cuban, Italian, and international options. Food quality is generally good by all-inclusive standards, though not comparable to the independent restaurant scenes in Grand Cayman or Providenciales.

**Outside the resorts:** Unlike Varadero (which has a small town with restaurants and paladares), Cayo Coco and Cayo Guillermo have virtually no independent dining infrastructure accessible to tourists. There are a handful of beach bars and small establishments, but you should plan on taking most meals at your resort.

### What to Eat Within Your Resort

**Cuban specialties to look for at the buffet and specialty restaurants:**
- **Ropa vieja** — shredded braised beef with tomatoes, peppers, and onion; the national dish of Cuba
- **Lechón asado** — slow-roasted suckling pig; a Cuban celebration staple
- **Moros y Cristianos** — black beans and white rice cooked together; appears at virtually every Cuban meal
- **Congrí** — rice cooked with red or black beans (regional variation of Moros y Cristianos)
- **Tostones** — twice-fried green plantain, served as a side or snack
- **Maduros** — sweet ripe fried plantain; more dessert-like than tostones
- **Ceviche and fresh fish** — the Caribbean waters around the Jardines del Rey are productive; fresh fish and seafood are usually the quality highlight at better resort restaurants
- **Mojito / Cuba Libre / Daiquiri** — Cuba's classic cocktails made properly are excellent at Cuban resorts; the mojito (rum, lime, sugar, mint, soda) served on its home island is particularly worth appreciating

### Outside the Resort Area

**El Pescador (Cayo Guillermo):** A modest seafood restaurant near the beach in Cayo Guillermo catering to visitors not in all-inclusive resorts; serves fresh fish and lobster. One of the few non-resort dining options in the area. Prices are in CUP/USD; expect approximately USD $15–$30/person.

**La Cueva del Jabalí (Cayo Coco):** A restaurant and nightclub set in an actual cave near the western end of Cayo Coco. Cuban food, music, and atmosphere in a genuinely unusual setting. Best experienced as an evening excursion from your resort; ask at your hotel's animation/entertainment desk for transportation. Not worth a dedicated trip for the food alone but the setting is memorable.

## Transportation

### CCC Airport to Hotel

Jardines del Rey Airport (CCC) is located on Cayo Coco, approximately **10–25 km from the resort hotels** depending on your specific property (Cayo Guillermo hotels are at the far end and take 20–25 minutes).

**Resort transfers:**
- The standard arrival experience in Cayo Coco is a **resort transfer included in your vacation package**. Air Canada Vacations, Sunwing, and Transat all include or offer transfers as part of their Cuba packages. Confirm your transfer booking before departure — it is the expected and most efficient option.
- If not included in your package, your resort can arrange a private transfer. Cost is approximately **USD $10–$25/vehicle** depending on distance.

**Taxis:**
- Official Cuban state taxis (Cubataxi) are available at the airport. Fares to resort areas are approximately **CUP equivalent of USD $10–$30**. Always confirm the fare in advance.
- Note: Independent taxi drivers (particularly those soliciting outside the airport) operate in a grey area of Cuban transportation regulations. Use official Cubataxi vehicles for safety and predictability.

### Getting Around the Cays

**Taxis:**
The primary option for inter-cay travel and excursions. Official Cubataxi vehicles can take you between resorts, to Playa Pilar from Cayo Coco hotels, to La Cueva del Jabalí, and to the flamingo viewing area. Fares are set and reasonable:
- Cayo Coco hotel to Playa Pilar: ~USD $15–$25 one-way
- Between resorts on the same cay: ~USD $5–$15
- Your resort's animation or excursion desk can arrange taxis and provide current rate guidance.

**Resort Shuttles:**
Many resorts offer shared shuttle services to Playa Pilar and other points of interest, especially on designated excursion days. Ask at the front desk or entertainment desk on arrival.

**Rental Cars:**
- Car rental is available at CCC airport and from some resorts through Cubacar (Cuban state car rental).
- A rental car gives you freedom to explore both cays, stop at flamingo observation points along the causeway, and visit Playa Pilar at your own pace.
- Rates: approximately **USD $60–$100/day** for a compact or SUV.
- **Cuba drives on the right side of the road** — same as Canada.
- **Important:** Road conditions, fuel availability, and rental vehicle quality in Cuba can be unpredictable. Fill the tank whenever fuel is available; gas stations on the cays are limited. Carry a paper map — GPS data reliability in rural Cuba is inconsistent.
- Cuban roads are generally safe from traffic but may have unmarked hazards (potholes, wandering animals, cyclists without lights). Drive cautiously, especially after dark.

**Scooter and Bicycle Rental:**
- Some resorts offer scooter and bicycle rental for exploring the immediate area. Suitable for short-range exploration within a cay.

## Attractions

### 1. Playa Pilar (Cayo Guillermo)
- **Hours:** Always accessible
- **Admission:** Free
- **Description:** Consistently rated one of **Cuba's finest beaches** and among the best in the Caribbean. Named after Ernest Hemingway's fishing boat (Hemingway fished the waters of Cayo Guillermo extensively — see *Islands in the Stream*), Playa Pilar features powder-white sand dunes reaching up to 16 metres in height — among the largest beach dunes in Cuba — and crystalline turquoise water. The shallow sandbar extends far offshore, creating conditions ideal for swimming, snorkelling, and paddling. The beach is well-maintained, not overcrowded (limited resort access), and strikingly beautiful. Even if staying at a Cayo Coco resort, a day trip to Playa Pilar is worth the taxi fare.
- **Tip:** Arrive before 10 AM for the calmest conditions and fewest other visitors. Bring your own shade if not staying at Iberostar Playa Pilar.

### 2. Wild Flamingo Observation — The Causeway and Interior Lagoons
- **Hours:** Best at dawn and dusk; accessible year-round
- **Admission:** Free (visible from the road/causeway)
- **Description:** Grand Cayman is famous for stingrays; Cayo Coco is famous for **flamingos**. The lagoon systems of Cayo Coco and the surrounding area support one of the largest wild flamingo colonies in Cuba — thousands of Caribbean flamingos feed in the shallow saline lagoons along the pedraplén causeway and in the interior wetlands. Unlike some "flamingo experiences" at other Caribbean destinations that involve captive birds, these are genuinely wild flocks moving freely through their natural habitat. You can observe them from the road, at dedicated pullout points along the causeway, or on guided flamingo tours offered by most resorts (approximately USD $25–$40/person).
- **Tip:** Bring binoculars for the best views. The flamingos are more reliably visible in the early morning or late afternoon when they are most active. The causeway drive itself — crossing the sea with lagoons on both sides — is scenic and worth doing at a slow pace.

### 3. Scuba Diving and Snorkelling — Jardines del Rey Reef System
- **Hours:** Dive operators typically depart 8–9 AM
- **Admission:** Snorkelling excursions ~USD $25–$45/person; diving ~USD $50–$90/person per tank
- **Description:** The Jardines del Rey archipelago sits above a pristine reef system with excellent visibility and abundant marine life. Coral health in this area is considered better than many other Cuban dive sites due to lower boat traffic and development. Common sightings include sea turtles, nurse sharks, barracuda, large schools of tropical fish, and varied coral gardens. The area's relative remoteness from Havana and Varadero has helped preserve its underwater environment.
- **Operators:** Most 4-5 star resorts have on-site dive centres (Meliá, Iberostar, and Pullman all operate diving programmes from Cayo Coco). The dive centres handle all gear, certification checks, and boat departures.
- **Tip:** Resort dive centres often bundle snorkelling excursions with catamaran day trips — ask about combo packages at check-in. Certification is not required for introductory dives; instructors handle first-time divers.

### 4. Catamaran and Sailing Excursions
- **Hours:** Typically depart 9 AM, return 3–4 PM
- **Admission:** ~USD $50–$80/person (often partially subsidized or included at all-inclusive resorts)
- **Description:** Half-day and full-day catamaran excursions are the signature daytime activity at Cayo Coco resorts. Boats sail to offshore snorkelling spots, uninhabited cays within the archipelago, and sandbanks in the shallow North Atlantic waters. Most excursions include snorkelling equipment, open bar (local rum, beer, soft drinks), and a simple lunch. The waters of the Jardines del Rey are extraordinarily clear in late April; underwater visibility during snorkelling is typically excellent.
- **Tip:** Book catamaran excursions through your resort's entertainment desk on arrival — they fill quickly in peak season. Morning departures tend to have better sea conditions than afternoon trips.

### 5. Deep-Sea Fishing
- **Hours:** Full-day charters typically depart 7 AM
- **Admission:** ~USD $200–$350/boat for a half-day charter (shared); full-day private charters ~USD $400–$600
- **Description:** The waters off the Jardines del Rey were extensively fished by Hemingway and remain productive for **blue marlin, swordfish, wahoo, mahi-mahi, and tarpon**. Deep-sea fishing charters operate from the marina areas near Cayo Guillermo and through resort excursion desks. For couples who enjoy sport fishing, this is a genuine draw — the combination of Hemingway's documented presence and the actual quality of the fishing makes it a meaningful excursion, not just a tourist activity.
- **Tip:** Fishing is best in the morning; book through your resort or directly through Marina Marlin Aguas Tranquilas (the state-operated marina near Cayo Guillermo).

### 6. Horseback Riding on the Beach
- **Hours:** Typically 8 AM–11 AM and 3–5 PM sessions
- **Admission:** ~USD $25–$45/person for a 1-hour beach ride
- **Description:** Horseback riding on the beach is offered by most resorts and through independent operators at Playa Pilar. A genuinely pleasant activity in the relatively flat, dry landscape of the cays; the early morning session along the shoreline before the heat of the day is particularly enjoyable.

### 7. Birding
- **Hours:** Best at dawn and dusk
- **Admission:** Free (accessible from resort grounds and roadsides)
- **Description:** The Jardines del Rey ecosystem supports exceptional birdlife beyond the famous flamingos. The wetlands and mangroves host **Cuban trogan** (Cuba's national bird), roseate spoonbills, pelicans, frigatebirds, ospreys, Cuban green woodpeckers, and a variety of migratory species passing through in late April. Dedicated birding walks are offered by some resorts; the flamingo observation points along the causeway are also excellent for seeing wading and shorebird species. Late April coincides with the spring migration period — excellent timing for bird diversity.

## Beaches

### Playa Pilar (Cayo Guillermo)
Cuba's most-celebrated beach and the showpiece of the archipelago. White dunes up to 16 metres high back a wide expanse of powder-white sand fronting shallow, brilliant turquoise water. The sandbar extends offshore in both directions, creating a vast swimming and wading zone with very calm conditions. Named after Hemingway's beloved fishing boat. Consistently ranked one of the top beaches in the Caribbean. Access is through Iberostar Playa Pilar resort or by taxi from other hotels.

### Playa Larga and Playa Centro (Cayo Coco)
The main beaches along Cayo Coco's northern shore, fronting the majority of the cay's resort hotels. White sand, clear warm water, and gentle conditions. Less spectacular than Playa Pilar in terms of the dune scenery, but excellent for swimming and watersports. Most Cayo Coco resort hotels sit directly on this stretch of beach.

### Playa Las Coloradas (Cayo Coco)
A quieter, more secluded beach section on the eastern side of Cayo Coco. Less developed than the main resort beach; the water is particularly clear here due to lower boat traffic. Accessible by rental car or taxi; some resorts offer shuttle service.

### Playa El Paso (Cayo Guillermo)
A sheltered beach cove on the interior (lagoon-facing) side of Cayo Guillermo, accessible from the Meliá Cayo Guillermo and nearby properties. Calm, shallow water; less exposed than the north-facing beaches. Good for kayaking and paddling in completely flat conditions.

## Practical Info

### April Weather

Late April in Cayo Coco falls at the tail end of Cuba's dry season — the best possible time to visit.

| Metric | Late April Average |
|---|---|
| Daytime high | 30°C (86°F) |
| Overnight low | 22°C (72°F) |
| Average temperature | 26°C (79°F) |
| Sea temperature | 27°C (80°F) |
| Humidity | ~65% (drier than summer months) |
| Rainfall | ~25–35 mm over ~3–4 days |
| Sunshine | ~9 hours/day |
| UV Index | 10–11 — **Very High to Extreme** |

- Late April is the final weeks of the dry season before the rainy season (May–October). Expect predominantly sunny and dry conditions; any rain tends to be brief.
- Trade winds from the northeast keep beach conditions comfortable; wind is stronger on the north-facing beaches (Playa Pilar, Playa Larga) and lighter in sheltered lagoon areas.
- Hurricane season begins June 1; late April has effectively no hurricane risk.
- Water temperature of 27°C is ideal for extended swimming and snorkelling without a wetsuit.

### Currency

Cuba's currency situation requires specific preparation:

- **Cuban Peso (CUP)** is the sole legal tender. The exchange rate floats; as of early 2026, approximately CUP 240–300 = USD 1 at official exchange (the informal market rate differs).
- **Best foreign currency to bring from Canada: Canadian dollars (CAD).** CAD is directly exchangeable at CADECA offices and resort front desks without penalty. You do not need to convert to USD first.
- **Avoid USD:** The US dollar incurs a 10% exchange penalty in Cuba; it is objectively worse to bring USD than CAD for a Canadian traveller.
- **Cash is king:** While some Cuban resort properties and hotel shops accept Canadian bank cards (Visa/Mastercard), card acceptance is unreliable. ATM availability at CCC airport and on the cays is limited and machines are frequently empty. **Bring sufficient CAD cash for the entire trip** — a good guideline is CAD $100–$200/day per couple beyond the all-inclusive package for tips, souvenirs, and excursions.
- **Tipping:** Tips are customarily in CUP or in small amounts of foreign currency (USD/CAD/EUR coins and small bills are welcome). See tipping section below.

### Entry Requirements for Canadians

- **Passport:** Valid Canadian passport required.
- **Cuban Tourist Card (visa):** All visitors to Cuba require a **tourist card** (sometimes called a "visa"). When flying with Air Canada or other Canadian carriers, the tourist card is typically included in the package or sold at check-in/at the airport for approximately **CAD $25–$35**. Confirm whether your ticket or package includes the tourist card — if not, purchase it at the Air Canada check-in desk at YYZ before boarding.
- **Cuban Health Insurance:** Cuba legally requires all tourists to have travel insurance covering health emergencies in Cuba. Most Canadian package vacations to Cuba include basic health insurance as part of the package (Sunwing, Air Canada Vacations, and Transat all include it). If booking independently, you must show proof of travel health insurance at immigration on arrival. Carry your policy documentation.
- **Return ticket:** Cuban immigration requires proof of onward travel (your return flight booking).

### Electricity

- **Voltage:** Cuba uses both **110V and 220V**. Most resort rooms have both voltage outlets.
- **Plugs:** 110V outlets use the same Type A and B plugs as Canada (identical to North American outlets). 220V outlets use different connectors — do not plug Canadian electronics into 220V outlets without a voltage adapter.
- **Practical advice:** Your Canadian phone charger, laptop, and standard electronics will work in the 110V outlets without any adapter. Be cautious with older hair dryers or appliances that specify voltage — check the label and use only on matching voltage.
- **Power interruptions:** While resorts in Cayo Coco generally have backup generators, brief power interruptions are part of Cuba's reality. Major resorts handle this seamlessly for guests; it is worth noting for context.

### Language

Spanish is the official language and is spoken universally. English is spoken by resort staff at all 4-5 star properties — communication at your hotel will be entirely in English. Outside the resort zone (on excursions or at local restaurants), basic Spanish phrases are helpful.

**Useful Spanish phrases:**
- *¿Cómo estás?* — How are you?
- *Gracias / De nada* — Thank you / You're welcome
- *¿Cuánto cuesta?* — How much does it cost?
- *Una cerveza, por favor* — A beer, please
- *La cuenta, por favor* — The bill, please

### Canada Travel Advisory

As of early 2026, the Government of Canada rates Cuba at **"Exercise a high degree of caution"** (Level 2). The advisory cites concerns including petty crime, economic instability, and the possibility of arbitrary detention in some circumstances. Tourist areas — including Cayo Coco and Cayo Guillermo — are considered lower-risk within Cuba's overall Level 2 rating; violent crime against tourists at the island resorts is rare.

**Practical safety at Cayo Coco:** The cays are isolated resort environments with limited public access; the risk profile for tourists staying within the resort zone is substantially lower than in Cuban cities. Standard travel precautions apply: keep valuables secured, be aware of your surroundings on excursions, and follow resort security guidance.

- Full advisory: [travel.gc.ca/destinations/cuba](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/cuba)

### Health Notes

- **No mandatory vaccinations** are required for Canadians visiting Cuba.
- **Cuban health insurance is legally required** (see Entry Requirements above — most packages include it).
- **Medical facilities on the cays:** Resort-level medical clinics handle minor emergencies. Serious conditions would require transfer to mainland Cuban facilities or medical evacuation.
- **Travel medical insurance beyond Cuban coverage:** Your package's included Cuban health insurance covers you within Cuba, but check whether it covers medical evacuation to Canada — standard travel medical insurance policies purchased separately (from your credit card or a travel insurer) provide broader coverage and are recommended.
- **Stomach sensitivity:** Some visitors experience mild digestive adjustment in Cuba. Drink only bottled water (provided free at resorts); use bottled water to brush teeth; avoid ice outside the resort. Well-managed resort restaurants maintain appropriate hygiene standards; risks are low at 4-5 star properties.
- **Sun protection:** UV Index 10–11 is Very High to Extreme. Apply SPF 50+ sunscreen, reapply every 90 minutes, wear a wide-brim hat, and seek shade between 11 AM–3 PM.
- **Mosquitoes:** Present in Cuba; dengue cases are reported. Use DEET-based insect repellent, especially at dawn and dusk in the lagoon/mangrove areas.

### Tipping Customs

Cuba's service workers are largely state employees with modest wages; tips are a meaningful supplement to their income and are deeply appreciated.

| Situation | Suggested Tip |
|---|---|
| Resort housekeeper | USD/CAD $1–$3/night, left daily |
| Buffet restaurant server | USD/CAD $1–$2 per meal |
| Specialty restaurant server | USD/CAD $3–$5 per couple |
| Bartender | USD/CAD $0.50–$1 per drink or $2–$3/session |
| Pool/beach attendant | USD/CAD $1–$2 per day |

| Taxi driver | Round up the fare; USD/CAD $2–$5 is appreciated |

- Small denomination USD, CAD, or EUR coins and bills are all welcome for tipping in Cuba.
- **Total tipping budget:** Budget approximately **CAD $80–$150 per couple** specifically for gratuities over a 5-night stay.

### 2SLGBTQI+ Travellers

Cuba's LGBTQ+ landscape has improved significantly in recent years:

- **Legal status:** Same-sex sexual relations are legal in Cuba. The **2022 Cuban Family Code**, passed by national referendum, legalized same-sex marriage and adoption — making Cuba one of the more progressive Caribbean and Latin American countries on LGBTQ+ legal rights.
- **In practice at resorts:** Resort staff at international-brand properties (Meliá, Iberostar, Accor) are professional and welcoming to all couples. Same-sex couples are a common presence at Cuban resort properties.
- **Social attitudes:** Cuba's urban centres (Havana in particular) have a relatively open LGBTQ+ scene. In resort areas like Cayo Coco, the atmosphere is more neutral — neither notably unwelcoming nor with dedicated LGBTQ+ infrastructure. Public displays of affection are generally accepted within the resort environment.
- The **Canada travel advisory** notes that some discrimination may occur but does not identify Cuba as a high-risk destination for LGBTQ+ travellers.
- **Bottom line:** For a resort-based beach vacation on the cays, Cayo Coco is a comfortable and low-concern destination for same-sex couples.
