## Flights

### Route & Schedule

Air Canada operates non-stop service between Toronto Pearson (YYZ) and Pointe-à-Pitre Le Raizet International Airport — also known as **Guadeloupe Pôle Caraïbes Airport** (PTP). As of early 2026, this route operates on **Saturdays only**, a seasonal schedule typical of Air Canada's less-frequented Caribbean destinations. This Saturday-only departure is a significant planning constraint: your outbound flight will be on a Saturday, and your return must align with available return dates (typically the following Saturday for a full week, or arrangements via Air Transat or Corsair for mid-week flexibility).

**Verify the schedule at time of booking.** Seasonal routes like YYZ–PTP can shift from year to year; late April 2026 falls within the spring season window when this service is typically active, but confirmation closer to booking is essential.

**Flight duration:** Approximately **5 hours** (YYZ to PTP). Return is similar.

**Terminal at YYZ:** All Air Canada flights depart from **Terminal 1** at Toronto Pearson International Airport.

**Estimated round-trip cost (2 passengers, economy):**
- Economy fares on YYZ–PTP typically range from approximately **CAD $500–$900/person** one-way, making a round trip approximately **CAD $1,000–$1,800 per person** all-in with taxes.
- For 2 passengers: budget approximately **CAD $2,000–$3,600 total** for economy round-trip flights in late April.
- **Air Transat** and **Corsair** (a French leisure carrier) also serve routes from Canada to Guadeloupe and are worth comparing — particularly if you want more schedule flexibility or a mid-week departure. Air Transat in particular has strong charter service to the French Caribbean from YYZ and YUL (Montreal).
- **Important scheduling note:** The Saturday-only Air Canada departure means your trip is anchored to Saturday outbound. Travellers wanting a Thursday or Sunday departure, or who need flexibility on return, should investigate Air Transat or Corsair options in parallel.

## Hotels

Guadeloupe's accommodation ranges from large beachfront all-inclusive resorts on the sandy east coast of Grande-Terre to intimate boutique guesthouses in the rainforest of Basse-Terre and charming small hotels on the outer island of Les Saintes. The island offers significantly better value than nearby Martinique or most British/US Caribbean destinations — genuinely good hotels at prices well below the premium Caribbean norm. French-influenced hospitality, excellent local food, and the diversity of the destination's geography make the accommodation choice particularly meaningful here: where you stay shapes what kind of island experience you have.

**Late April note:** Guadeloupe's high season runs December through April. Late April is at the very end of peak season — most resorts are still operating at near-peak rates but availability is improving. Book 2–3 months ahead for the best room types at the top properties.

### 1. Club Med La Caravelle (All-Inclusive)
- **Area:** Sainte-Anne, Grande-Terre (directly on Plage de la Caravelle — the finest beach on the main island)
- **Category:** 5-trident (Club Med's top tier), adults-oriented all-inclusive (families also welcome)
- **Price range (5 nights, 2 people):** Approximately **CAD $3,000–$5,500** depending on room category and booking window
- **Key amenities:** One of Club Med's flagship Caribbean resorts and among the most celebrated in the entire Club Med portfolio. Direct beachfront on Plage de la Caravelle (a genuinely exceptional stretch of white sand). All meals and open bar included, watersports (sailing, windsurfing, snorkelling, kayaking, paddleboarding), tennis, archery, fitness centre, nightly entertainment and shows, multiple restaurants and bars. The beach setting and quality of the facility are outstanding.
- **Best for:** Couples who want a true all-inclusive on Guadeloupe's best beach with no surprise costs. The Sainte-Anne location puts you on Grande-Terre's southern coast, close to the island's most developed tourist infrastructure while retaining a genuine beach-resort feel. One of the most popular options for Canadians visiting Guadeloupe.
- **Note:** Adults-oriented atmosphere; Club Med's "Club" category here skews toward couples and social groups. Book through Club Med directly or via Air Canada Vacations for package pricing.

### 2. La Créole Beach Hôtel & Spa
- **Area:** Gosier, Grande-Terre (the main tourist resort town east of Pointe-à-Pitre)
- **Category:** 4-star full-service beach resort
- **Price range (5 nights, 2 people):** Approximately **USD $900–$1,750** (room only; ~USD $180–$350/night)
- **Key amenities:** Beachfront location on the Gosier lagoon, multiple pools, full-service spa, several restaurants including a seafood terrace, bar, watersports, direct beach access, fitness centre. One of the best-known mid-to-upper-range hotels on the island. French Caribbean atmosphere with professional service.
- **Best for:** Couples who want a proper hotel experience on Grande-Terre with good beach access, solid dining on-site, and proximity to Gosier's restaurants and nightlife, without the all-inclusive format. Central location makes island day trips convenient. Good value by Caribbean standards.
- **Note:** Gosier is the most developed tourist area on the island — lively, with beach bars, restaurants, and the islet of Gosier (a short swim or kayak offshore) as a focal point.

### 3. La Toubana Hôtel & Spa
- **Area:** Sainte-Anne, Grande-Terre (hillside cliffside setting overlooking the bay)
- **Category:** 4–5 star boutique hillside resort
- **Price range (5 nights, 2 people):** Approximately **USD $1,000–$2,000** (room only; ~USD $200–$400/night)
- **Key amenities:** Individual bungalows and suites cascading down a hillside above the Sainte-Anne coastline, each with terrace and sweeping sea views; most units have private plunge pools. Full-service spa, a highly regarded restaurant and bar with panoramic views, pool, and shuttle to the beach below. One of Guadeloupe's most romantic and design-forward properties.
- **Best for:** Couples celebrating an anniversary, honeymoon, or special occasion who want seclusion, excellent food, breathtaking views, and a genuinely intimate atmosphere. The plunge-pool bungalows are among the most romantic rooms on the island.
- **Note:** The property is on a hillside — it is not beachfront, but its shuttle to the beach and the dramatic elevated setting more than compensate. A rental car is helpful from this property for excursions.

### 4. Karibéa Sainte-Anne
- **Area:** Sainte-Anne, Grande-Terre (near the beach)
- **Category:** 3–4 star; solid value property
- **Price range (5 nights, 2 people):** Approximately **USD $600–$1,000** (room only; ~USD $120–$200/night)
- **Key amenities:** Pool, gardens, proximity to Sainte-Anne beach and the town's restaurants and shops, comfortable standard rooms. A practical, well-located mid-range option without the premium resort price tag.
- **Best for:** Budget-conscious couples who want a comfortable base in the Sainte-Anne area — one of the island's best beach zones — with easy access to the beach and a walkable town, while spending their food budget at the excellent local restaurants nearby rather than paying for all-inclusive.
- **Note:** Sainte-Anne town has an excellent Saturday morning market and numerous very good Creole restaurants within walking distance. Good value for independent travellers.

### 5. Hôtel Les Petits Saints
- **Area:** Terre-de-Haut, Les Saintes (the outer island archipelago — requires a ferry from Grande-Terre)
- **Category:** Boutique luxury; small property on one of the Caribbean's most beautiful islands
- **Price range (5 nights, 2 people):** Approximately **USD $1,000–$1,750** (room only; ~USD $200–$350/night)
- **Key amenities:** A small, elegantly decorated boutique hotel on the island of Terre-de-Haut in Les Saintes archipelago. Pool with views, excellent on-site restaurant, intimate personal service, a genuine sense of Caribbean elegance removed from mass tourism. Surrounded by one of the most beautiful natural harbours in the world.
- **Best for:** Couples who want an immersive experience on Les Saintes — often described as one of the most beautiful spots in the entire Caribbean — rather than the main island resort scene. Staying here puts you in the village of Bourg des Saintes, a beautifully preserved French-Caribbean settlement with superb fresh seafood, beaches, and hiking.
- **Note:** Getting here requires a ferry from either Trois-Rivières (Basse-Terre side, ~30 min) or Pointe-à-Pitre. This is the most logistically involved option but many visitors say Les Saintes is the highlight of any Guadeloupe trip. Consider combining 2–3 nights here with 2–3 nights on the main island.

### 6. Ti Kaz La
- **Area:** Basse-Terre (the western, mountainous, rainforest-covered island)
- **Category:** Boutique guesthouse; ecotourism-oriented
- **Price range (5 nights, 2 people):** Approximately **USD $500–$900** (room only; ~USD $100–$180/night)
- **Key amenities:** A small, intimate guesthouse set in the lush rainforest interior of Basse-Terre. Excellent home-cooked Creole breakfasts, attentive hosts, a quiet natural setting with bird life and tropical vegetation. Simple but characterful accommodation that functions as a base for exploring the Parc National de la Guadeloupe, La Soufrière volcano, and the Chutes du Carbet waterfalls.
- **Best for:** Couples who are as interested in Guadeloupe's extraordinary natural landscape as its beaches — hikers, nature lovers, and those who want to experience the quieter, greener side of the island. Excellent value for the experience offered.
- **Note:** Basse-Terre is a meaningfully different experience from the resort-beach side of Grande-Terre — more rugged, more local, more French Caribbean in character. A rental car is essential from this property. Not suitable as the sole base if beach time is the primary goal.

> **Booking tip:** Guadeloupe is less frequently bundled in Canadian mass-market vacation packages than destinations like Cuba or the Dominican Republic, but **Air Canada Vacations** and **Air Transat Holidays** do offer packages to Club Med La Caravelle and some Grande-Terre properties and are worth checking. Independent bookings via Booking.com and direct hotel websites are standard for the boutique and mid-range properties. As a French territory, some hotels primarily serve a French and European clientele — English-language booking platforms are available but note that on-property communication may be primarily in French.

## Dining

French-Creole cuisine is one of Guadeloupe's defining pleasures — a sophisticated fusion of classical French technique with African, Indigenous, and East Indian culinary traditions that arrived with the island's complex history. The food is exceptionally good by Caribbean standards: freshly caught seafood, aromatic spice blends, rich stews, exceptional rum, and the deep flavours of traditional Creole cooking.

**The signature flavour of Guadeloupe is the colombo** — a spice blend brought by Indian indentured workers in the 19th century, similar to curry but distinctly local, used to season chicken, goat, fish, and vegetables. It appears on virtually every Creole menu.

**Late April context:** The week leading up to and following Easter in late April is one of the most food-significant times on the Guadeloupean calendar. **Matoutou de crabe** — crab with rice, a traditional springtime dish — is consumed in enormous quantities at Easter and throughout April. If you visit in late April, you will find it on menus everywhere; try it.

**Budget note:** Dining in Guadeloupe is very reasonable by Caribbean standards. A full sit-down Creole lunch (the main meal of the day in French Caribbean culture) typically runs EUR $15–$30/person. Dinner at a good restaurant is EUR $25–$50/person. Only the top restaurant tables approach the prices common in Turks & Caicos or Grand Cayman.

### Dishes and Drinks to Know

**Accras de morue** — Salt cod fritters, lightly spiced and deep fried. The ubiquitous Guadeloupean starter; served at virtually every Creole restaurant. Order them immediately.

**Colombo** — The island's signature curry-style dish. Chicken colombo and goat colombo are the most common preparations; fish colombo is also excellent. Aromatic, fragrant, and deeply satisfying. Not identical to Indian curry — the colombo spice blend is its own thing.

**Blaff** — Fish or shellfish poached in a fragrant broth of lime juice, garlic, chilli, thyme, and bay leaf. Clean, delicate, and very Creole. Particularly good with local langouste (spiny lobster) or fresh fish.

**Matoutou de crabe** — Traditional Easter dish of crab cooked with rice, tomatoes, and Creole seasoning. A cultural institution in late April; not to be missed during a spring visit.

**Boudin créole** — Spiced Creole blood sausage, served hot. A street food staple and market snack; much better than it sounds to the uninitiated.

**Oursins (sea urchin)** — Served raw, split open, and eaten with a squeeze of lime. A local delicacy available at beachside vendors and seafood restaurants. An adventurous but genuinely excellent experience for those inclined to try it.

**Rhum agricole and Ti' Punch** — Guadeloupean rum is among the finest in the world. Unlike industrial rum made from molasses, **rhum agricole** is distilled directly from fresh-pressed sugarcane juice, producing a cleaner, more complex spirit. The local aperitif ritual is the **Ti' Punch** (petit punch): rhum agricole, a little cane syrup, and a squeeze of lime — stirred, not shaken, and consumed at any hour of the day. Every bar, restaurant, and home serves Ti' Punch. Order one at your first Creole lunch.

## Transportation

### Airport to Hotel

Pointe-à-Pitre Le Raizet International Airport (PTP) is located in the Pointe-à-Pitre urban area, on the isthmus connecting Grande-Terre and Basse-Terre.

**Taxis:**
- Taxis are available at the airport arrivals area and operate on regulated fares.
- To Gosier (the main resort area east of Pointe-à-Pitre): approximately **EUR $20–$30** per vehicle.
- To Sainte-Anne (the beach resort zone further east on Grande-Terre): approximately **EUR $35–$50** per vehicle.
- Fares increase after 7:00 PM and on Sundays and public holidays. Confirm the fare before departing. Carry EUR cash; not all taxis accept cards.

**Car rental (recommended at the airport):**
- Collecting a rental car at the airport on arrival is the most practical approach for most visitors. All major agencies are represented. See Getting Around section below.

**Note:** There is no reliable tourist shuttle or public bus service from PTP airport. Arrange a taxi or rental car.

### Getting Around

**Car rental is strongly recommended for Guadeloupe — this cannot be emphasised enough.** Unlike a small all-inclusive island such as Barbados or a compact resort strip like Cancún, Guadeloupe is a genuinely large, geographically diverse destination spanning two main islands plus outer islands. The public bus network (shared minibuses) serves the main towns but is oriented toward local commuters, not tourists making day trips to La Soufrière or Malendure. Without a car, your world shrinks to the immediate resort area. With a car, the entire island opens up.

- **Rental rates:** Approximately **EUR $40–$70/day** for a compact car in late April from airport agencies (Avis, Europcar, Hertz, Sixt, local operators).
- **Drive on the RIGHT** — Guadeloupe follows French road rules and right-hand traffic. Canadian drivers will be immediately comfortable.
- A valid Canadian driver's licence is accepted. Minimum age is typically 21; under-25 surcharges may apply.
- Roads on Grande-Terre are generally good. Roads on Basse-Terre, particularly in the national park interior and on the approach to La Soufrière, become narrower and steeper — drive carefully in these areas.
- **Fuel:** Gas stations are found in main towns. Fill up before heading into the Basse-Terre interior for a full day of exploration.

**Ferry services between islands:**
- **Trois-Rivières (Basse-Terre) to Terre-de-Haut (Les Saintes):** ~30 minutes; approximately **EUR $15/person round trip**. Multiple daily departures; the most convenient ferry for a Les Saintes day trip from Basse-Terre.
- **Pointe-à-Pitre to Terre-de-Haut (Les Saintes):** ~60 minutes; a longer option from the Grande-Terre side.
- **Pointe-à-Pitre to Marie-Galante:** ~45 minutes by fast ferry.
- Ferry services are operated by several companies including L'Express des Îles and CTM Deher. Schedules vary seasonally — check locally for current times. Booking ahead for peak days (Saturdays, holidays) is advisable.

**Taxis (on-island):**
- Taxis are available in all towns but are expensive for longer trips and do not replace a rental car for touring the island efficiently.
- For short hops within Gosier or Sainte-Anne, taxis are convenient. For Basse-Terre, a rental car is the only practical option.

## Attractions

### 1. La Soufrière Volcano (Basse-Terre)
- **Hours:** Trails generally open daylight hours; guided hike departures typically begin 7–8 AM
- **Admission:** Free to hike independently; guided hikes ~EUR $30–$50/person
- **Description:** The centrepiece of Basse-Terre and one of the most dramatic experiences in the entire Caribbean. La Soufrière is an active stratovolcano rising to **1,467 metres (4,813 feet)** — the highest point in the Lesser Antilles. The summit hike ascends through tropical cloud forest, past sulphurous fumaroles venting steam and volcanic gases, to a crater rim with views across Basse-Terre when cloud permits. The landscape near the summit is genuinely otherworldly: grey volcanic rock, yellow sulphur deposits, clouds of steam, and silence broken only by wind and earth. The round-trip hike takes **4–6 hours** from the main trailhead at Savane à Mulets; allow a full day. Guided hikes are strongly recommended for first-timers — guides provide context, navigate safely around active venting areas, and are required in some zones depending on volcanic activity levels.
- **Tip:** La Soufrière's summit is frequently in cloud, particularly in the afternoon. Start early (7 AM) for the best chance of clearer conditions at the top. Check volcanic activity status before heading up — the volcano is active and occasionally restricts access. Wear waterproof hiking boots and bring a rain layer; conditions change rapidly in the cloud forest.

### 2. Réserve Cousteau / Malendure (Basse-Terre)
- **Hours:** Snorkel and dive operators typically depart 8 AM–2 PM
- **Admission:** Snorkelling from the beach: free; snorkelling boat tours ~EUR $20–$35/person; scuba diving ~EUR $45–$70/person for a 2-tank dive
- **Description:** One of the finest snorkelling and diving destinations in the entire Caribbean. The **Réserve Naturelle du Cousteau** — a protected marine reserve off Malendure beach on Basse-Terre's west coast — was established in part through the advocacy of Jacques Cousteau, who famously described these waters as "one of the ten best dive sites in the world." The reserve protects a rich ecosystem of healthy coral, sea turtles (extremely common — encounters are virtually guaranteed), sea horses, moray eels, barracuda, and abundant reef fish. Visibility frequently exceeds 30 metres. The shallow areas are accessible to snorkellers directly from Malendure beach or by short boat ride; dive operators (including Pisquettes and other established local shops) offer guided dives to deeper sites including the Pain de Sucre and the Sec Pâté.
- **Tip:** Unlike many "famous" dive sites that have been loved to degradation, the Cousteau Reserve benefits from strong protection and remains genuinely exceptional. Even moderate snorkellers will see an astonishing amount of marine life. Plan at least a half day here; combine with lunch at Le Rocher de Malendure above the beach.

### 3. Les Saintes (Îles des Saintes)
- **Hours:** Ferry departures vary; plan for a full day or overnight
- **Admission:** Ferry fare ~EUR $15/person round trip from Trois-Rivières
- **Description:** A small archipelago of eight islands, of which **Terre-de-Haut** is the main inhabited one, located about 15 kilometres south of Basse-Terre. Les Saintes is consistently cited as one of the most beautiful places in the entire Caribbean — a claim well-supported on arrival. The bay of Terre-de-Haut is widely considered one of the most beautiful natural harbours in the world, with the emerald-green island of La Cabrit in the centre and green hills on all sides. The village of **Bourg des Saintes** is a perfectly preserved French-Caribbean settlement of narrow streets, pastel-coloured houses, excellent seafood restaurants, a small beach (Anse du Bourg), and an almost complete absence of mass tourism. Fort Napoléon, perched on the hillside above the town, offers panoramic views over the bay. The local speciality is the **tourment d'amour** — a coconut tart sold at every bakery in the village.
- **Tip:** Les Saintes is best experienced as an overnight stay (Hôtel Les Petits Saints or similar) rather than a rushed day trip. If you can only day-trip, take the early ferry from Trois-Rivières, walk to the fort, eat lunch at Chez Prudence, swim at Anse du Bourg, and return on an evening ferry. Rent a scooter or small electric car on the island (available at the pier) to reach quieter beaches beyond the village.

### 4. Chutes du Carbet (Basse-Terre)
- **Hours:** Trails accessible during daylight hours; arrive early
- **Admission:** Small parking/access fee ~EUR $3–$5
- **Description:** Three dramatic waterfalls cascading down the slopes of the national park on Basse-Terre. The most visited is the **Second Fall (Deuxième Chute)** — a 110-metre cascade accessible via a moderate hiking trail of approximately 1 hour each way through lush tropical forest. The trail passes through fern gullies, bamboo groves, and past numerous smaller streams. The falls themselves are genuinely impressive — a thin curtain of white water plunging from the volcanic escarpment above into a pool surrounded by tropical vegetation. The Third Fall is shorter and reached more easily; the First Fall (115 metres) is the tallest but requires a more challenging full-day hike.
- **Tip:** Wear proper hiking shoes — the trails are muddy and slippery even in dry conditions. Bring insect repellent and rain gear. Morning visits are recommended before clouds build up over the mountains.

### 5. Parc National de la Guadeloupe
- **Hours:** Park accessible year-round; visitor centre in Matouba has regular hours
- **Admission:** Free (some trails have small parking fees)
- **Description:** The national park covers most of the interior of Basse-Terre — approximately 17,300 hectares of tropical rainforest, volcanic terrain, rivers, waterfalls, and cloud forest. The **Route de la Traversée** (N1 road) cuts east-west across Basse-Terre through the heart of the park, passing through cathedral-like mahogany forest with signed stops for waterfalls, viewpoints, and short nature trails. The **Maison de la Forêt** visitor centre along this route explains the park's ecosystems with interpretive displays. Beyond La Soufrière and the Chutes du Carbet, the park contains dozens of marked hiking trails through varied terrain. The juxtaposition of Guadeloupe's beaches and rainforest — within an hour's drive of each other — is one of the island's most compelling features.
- **Tip:** Even if you're not planning a major hike, a half-day drive along the Route de la Traversée from Grande-Terre through the park to the west coast, stopping at the viewpoints and the Cascade aux Ecrevisses (a short walk to a small waterfall into a swimming pool), is one of the best scenic drives in the Caribbean.

### 6. Marché de la Darse and Pointe-à-Pitre City (Grande-Terre)
- **Hours:** Market busiest Tuesday–Saturday mornings; Sunday is quiet
- **Admission:** Free
- **Description:** Pointe-à-Pitre is Guadeloupe's main commercial city and port — not a tourist destination per se, but containing one of the Caribbean's most vibrant and authentic covered markets. The **Marché de la Darse** and the adjacent **Marché Saint-Antoine** are where Guadeloupeans buy their spices, tropical fruits (soursop, starfruit, plantain, passion fruit), fresh flowers, rum, and prepared Creole food. The atmosphere is lively, fragrant, and entirely local. Excellent for purchasing souvenir spices — **colombo powder** (blended and sold loose by the spice vendors), vanilla, dried chilli, and small bottles of local rum make ideal and inexpensive gifts. The **Mémorial ACTe** — a powerful and striking museum of the history of slavery and the Atlantic slave trade — is also located in Pointe-à-Pitre at the former Darboussier sugar refinery and is worth a half-day visit.
- **Tip:** Visit the market in the morning and combine with the Mémorial ACTe for a full Pointe-à-Pitre day. Be aware of your belongings in busy market areas — petty theft is possible in crowded urban spaces.

### 7. Plage de la Caravelle and the Sainte-Anne Coast (Grande-Terre)
- **Hours:** Always accessible; beach bars/restaurants open from mid-morning
- **Admission:** Free (Club Med section is resort guests only; public sections are at the beach ends)
- **Description:** The finest beach on the main island and one of the best in the Caribbean. A broad arc of white sand on Grande-Terre's southern coast, protected by a coral reef that keeps the water calm and clear. The beach fronts Club Med La Caravelle; public access points are at both ends. The wider Sainte-Anne coast offers multiple beaches and coves, with the town's beach bars and seafood restaurants making it the social heart of Grande-Terre's beach scene.
- **Tip:** The Sainte-Anne area rewards a full day — morning snorkelling off the reef, a long Creole lunch at Le Karacoli or a beach roulette, and an afternoon in a beach chair watching the water change colour. Saturday morning brings the lively Sainte-Anne market.

## Beaches

### Plage de la Caravelle (Sainte-Anne, Grande-Terre)
The finest beach on the main island. A wide crescent of luminous white sand on Grande-Terre's southern coast, sheltered by a coral reef that keeps the water calm, clear, and shallow — perfect for swimming, snorkelling, and wading. The water is an exceptional turquoise-to-blue gradient. Club Med La Caravelle fronts the centre of the beach; public access is unrestricted at both ends. The combination of beach quality, reef snorkelling, and proximity to Sainte-Anne's restaurants makes this the hub of Grande-Terre beach life. Sea temperature in late April is approximately **27°C (80°F)**.

### Plage du Souffleur (Port-Louis, Grande-Terre)
Often cited as one of the most beautiful beaches in Guadeloupe. Located on the northern coast of Grande-Terre near the town of Port-Louis, Plage du Souffleur is a long sweep of white sand lined with graceful coconut palms, with calm, clear water and a fraction of the crowds found at Sainte-Anne. The town of Port-Louis has a good local fish market and Saturday market. A rental car is needed from the resort areas — approximately 45 minutes from Gosier. Worth the drive.

### Plage de Saint-François (Saint-François, Grande-Terre)
A wide, calm beach at the eastern tip of Grande-Terre, in the town of Saint-François — a pleasant resort village with a marina, casino, and good restaurants. The beach is long, gently shelving, and excellent for swimming. The lagoon waters between Saint-François and the outer island of La Désirade are a popular kitesurfing zone. The town has a lively Friday night market. Approximately 30 minutes east of Sainte-Anne.

### Malendure Beach (Bouillante, Basse-Terre)
A distinctive dark-sand beach — volcanic in origin — on Basse-Terre's leeward coast, directly across from the Réserve Cousteau marine reserve. The beach itself is modest in purely aesthetic terms compared to the white-sand beaches of Grande-Terre, but its significance as the entry point for the best snorkelling and diving in Guadeloupe makes it essential. Multiple dive operators are based directly on the beach. After a morning in the water, lunch at Le Rocher de Malendure above the beach is the natural sequel. The combination of exceptional marine life, good food, and a dramatic Basse-Terre mountain backdrop makes this one of the most memorable beach experiences on the island.

### Anse du Bourg (Terre-de-Haut, Les Saintes)
The postcard beach of Les Saintes, and one of the most photographed settings in the French Caribbean. A crescent of pale sand at the foot of Bourg des Saintes village, framed by the island's green hills on all sides and the turquoise waters of the bay. The beach is fronted by the village's restaurants and beach bars — you can swim, eat fresh lobster, and watch the fishing boats come in without moving more than 100 metres. Calm, clear, and completely charming. Best experienced as part of an overnight on Les Saintes rather than a rushed day trip.

## Practical Info

### April Weather

Late April in Guadeloupe falls at the transition point between the dry season (carême) and the beginning of the wetter season (hivernage). Conditions on Grande-Terre are excellent for beach holidaymakers. Basse-Terre, due to its mountainous terrain capturing trade wind moisture, is significantly wetter and more lushly vegetated year-round.

| Metric | Late April Average |
|---|---|
| Daytime high | 29°C (84°F) |
| Overnight low | 23°C (73°F) |
| Sea temperature | 27°C (80°F) |
| Sunshine | ~9 hours/day |
| Rainfall (Grande-Terre) | ~50 mm over ~5 days |
| Rainfall (Basse-Terre) | Higher — expect afternoon showers in the mountains |
| Humidity | ~70–75% |
| UV Index | 11–12 — **Extreme** |

- Late April is at the end of Guadeloupe's high season — warm, generally sunny, with the trade winds keeping the heat comfortable on the beach. Brief afternoon showers are possible but typically clear quickly on Grande-Terre.
- **Basse-Terre caveat:** If you are planning hikes to La Soufrière or the Chutes du Carbet, pack a lightweight waterproof jacket. The cloud forest above 700 metres is frequently wet even on sunny days. Volcanic peaks can be completely cloud-capped. Start hikes early.
- **UV warning:** A UV Index of 11–12 is classified as extreme — unprotected skin can burn in as little as 10 minutes at midday. Apply SPF 50+ sunscreen before going out and reapply every 90 minutes. Wear a broad-brimmed hat and UV-blocking sunglasses, particularly during beach and open-terrain days.
- Hurricane season officially begins June 1; late April carries essentially no hurricane risk.

### Currency

The **Euro (EUR)** is the official currency of Guadeloupe. As a French overseas department and full member of the Eurozone, Guadeloupe uses the Euro in exactly the same way as France or any EU country. **Canadian dollars and US dollars are not accepted anywhere** — not at hotels, restaurants, shops, taxis, or markets. This is non-negotiable.

- **Exchange CAD to EUR before departing Canada.** Banks, major currency exchange services (Calforex, ICE), and airport kiosks in Toronto offer EUR. Note that airport exchange rates are typically less favourable than bank or Calforex rates — exchange the majority of your EUR before arriving at YYZ.
- **Credit and debit cards** (Visa, Mastercard) are accepted at all hotels, most restaurants, and shops in tourist areas. Inform your bank before travel to avoid fraud holds on international transactions.
- **ATMs (DABs)** are widely available in Pointe-à-Pitre, Gosier, Sainte-Anne, and Saint-François. They dispense EUR. Canadian debit cards on the Cirrus/Plus network will work; check with your bank regarding international withdrawal fees (typically CAD $3–$5 per transaction plus possible currency conversion fees).
- As a rough guide, budget approximately **EUR $80–$150 per couple per day** for food, local transportation, activities, and incidentals, exclusive of accommodation. This is genuinely good value compared to most Caribbean destinations.

### Electricity

- **Voltage:** 220V, 50 Hz — European standard; significantly different from Canada's 120V, 60 Hz.
- **Plugs:** Type E (French/European — round two-pin with a grounding pin). Completely different from Canadian plugs.
- **Canadian visitors need BOTH a voltage converter AND a plug adapter.** This is important: most Canadian electronics (phone chargers, laptops) are dual-voltage (100–240V) and will work fine with only a plug adapter, but older appliances and some hair dryers or straighteners are single-voltage (120V only) and will be damaged or destroyed if plugged in without a voltage converter. Check the label on every device before plugging it in. When in doubt, buy a 220V travel hair dryer locally or at a travel shop in Toronto before departure.
- Modern smartphones, laptops, tablets, and most travel electronics sold in the past 10 years are dual-voltage and safe with a plug adapter only. The adapter itself (Type E) is inexpensive (~CAD $10–$15) and widely available at Canadian electronics and travel shops.

### Language

**French** is the official language of Guadeloupe. **Guadeloupean Creole** (Antillean Creole, a French-based creole language) is widely spoken alongside French, particularly in markets, between locals, and in informal settings.

**English is not widely spoken outside the main tourist resorts.** This is a French department with a French-oriented visitor base — unlike Turks & Caicos or Barbados, where English is universal and Canadians feel entirely at home linguistically. At Club Med and the major international hotels, English-speaking staff are available. At local restaurants, markets, guesthouses, and in day-to-day interaction, French is the working language.

**Basic French goes a long way.** Learning a handful of essentials — *Bonjour*, *S'il vous plaît*, *Merci*, *L'addition, s'il vous plaît* (the bill, please), *Deux personnes* (two people), *Un Ti' Punch, s'il vous plaît* — will be genuinely appreciated and will open doors. Guadeloupeans are warm and welcoming but respond better when visitors make an effort in French than when they assume English will be understood.

**Practical tip:** Download the Google Translate app with French available offline before departing. Restaurant menus are in French; having a translation tool at hand is useful.

### Canada Travel Advisory

As of early 2026, the Government of Canada rates Guadeloupe at **"Exercise Normal Security Precautions"** — the lowest advisory level. Guadeloupe is generally considered a safe destination for tourists. Being a French department with European legal and law enforcement systems, it has a different security profile than many of its Caribbean neighbours. The advisory contains no specific crime warnings for tourist areas.

- Petty theft (pickpocketing, bag snatching) can occur in busy urban areas, particularly around Pointe-à-Pitre's markets and the port. Take standard precautions — do not display valuables, secure your bag, and be aware of your surroundings in the city.
- The island has experienced periodic labour and social unrest associated with French territorial politics and economic inequality — these occasionally result in road blockages (barrages). If travelling by car on Basse-Terre in particular, check local conditions. These disruptions are typically non-violent but can delay travel plans significantly.
- Always consult **travel.gc.ca** for the latest advisory before departure.

### Health Notes

- **No mandatory vaccinations** are required for entry to Guadeloupe for Canadian citizens. It is a French department with European health standards.
- **Routine vaccinations** should be current: COVID-19, influenza, MMR, tetanus/diphtheria.
- **Hepatitis A** vaccination is recommended as a standard travel precaution for any Caribbean destination.
- **Medical facilities:** Guadeloupe has the **CHU de Pointe-à-Pitre** (Centre Hospitalier Universitaire) — a well-equipped French university hospital that is the main medical facility in the French Caribbean. Medical standards meet French European norms. Serious conditions can also be evacuated to metropolitan France if required.
- **Travel medical insurance is essential.** Canadian provincial health plans (OHIP, etc.) provide no meaningful coverage outside Canada. A policy covering emergency medical treatment, hospitalization, and evacuation is strongly recommended. Guadeloupe's healthcare is considerably more robust than most Caribbean island facilities, but the cost of medical care billed to uninsured foreign patients can still be substantial.
- **Sun and UV:** The primary health risk for beach visitors. Apply SPF 50+ sunscreen generously and reapply frequently. Seek shade during peak sun hours (11 AM–3 PM). The UV Index of 11–12 in late April is extreme.
- **Mosquitoes and dengue:** Aedes mosquitoes are present in Guadeloupe; dengue fever outbreaks occur periodically. Apply DEET-based repellent, particularly at dawn and dusk. The Zika virus has also been present in Guadeloupe historically — consult your doctor if there are pregnancy considerations.
- **Water:** Tap water in Guadeloupe meets French EU drinking water standards and is generally safe to drink. Bottled water is widely available if preferred.

### Tipping Customs

Guadeloupe follows **French tipping culture**, which is meaningfully different from North American conventions. Tipping is appreciated but not expected or obligatory in the way it is in the United States and Canada. Service charges (service compris) are typically included in restaurant bills as required by French law.

| Situation | Suggested Practice |
|---|---|
| Restaurant (service compris on bill) | Rounding up or leaving EUR $1–$3 for good service is appreciated; not obligatory |
| Restaurant (service NOT included) | EUR $3–$5 is generous and welcomed |
| Hotel housekeeper | EUR $1–$2/night left at checkout; not a rigid expectation |
| Taxi driver | Rounding up the fare (e.g., EUR $32 fare → leave EUR $35) is common |
| Tour guide / dive operator | EUR $5–$10/person for a half-day guided experience is appreciated |
| Bar staff | Not expected; rounding up loosely is fine |

- **Always check your restaurant bill:** In French establishments, *service compris* (service included) means the gratuity is already built into the prices. Adding a North American-style 20% tip on top of this would be unnecessary, though a small gesture for exceptional service is always welcome.
- Overall, tipping is considerably less financially significant here than in USD-based Caribbean destinations. Budget approximately **EUR $30–$60 per couple** for gratuities over a 5-night stay — a fraction of what TCI or Grand Cayman would require.

### Entry Requirements for Canadians

- **Passport:** A valid Canadian passport is required. There is no minimum validity requirement beyond the travel dates under standard French/EU entry rules, but the general travel recommendation is at least 6 months of validity beyond the return date.
- **Visa:** Not required. Guadeloupe is a French overseas department — it is legally part of France and the European Union. Canadian citizens may enter for tourism stays without a visa (as with metropolitan France). There is no separate Guadeloupe immigration process; arrival formalities are standard French territory procedures.
- **No tourist card** or landing card is required — unlike many British Caribbean territories. Arrivals are processed through French/EU immigration.
- **Return ticket:** Customs officers may ask to confirm onward travel arrangements. Have your return booking confirmation accessible.
- **Time zone:** Guadeloupe observes **Atlantic Standard Time (AST) — UTC−4 year-round**, without daylight saving time. In late April, Toronto is on EDT (UTC−4). This means Guadeloupe and Toronto are **on the same time** during late April — zero time change, no jet lag adjustment required. This is a convenient practical advantage.

### 2SLGBTQI+ Travellers

Guadeloupe offers a welcoming environment for 2SLGBTQI+ travellers, reflecting its status as a French overseas department where French law applies in full.

- **Legal status:** French law applies — same-sex marriage has been legal in France (and therefore in Guadeloupe) since 2013. Same-sex couples have full legal equality under French law, including marriage, adoption, and anti-discrimination protections. There are no legal concerns for LGBTQ+ travellers.
- **In practice at resorts:** The major international resort properties — Club Med La Caravelle, La Toubana, La Créole Beach — cater to international visitors and welcome all couples. The French Caribbean has a broadly liberal and inclusive attitude consistent with metropolitan France.
- **In local communities:** The French Antilles remain predominantly Catholic with some socially conservative community norms, particularly in smaller towns and rural areas. Public displays of affection between same-sex couples are unlikely to cause confrontation but may draw attention outside the main tourist areas. In practice, the French cultural norm of discretion in public (which applies equally to all couples) means most visitors experience no difficulties.
- The **Canada travel advisory** contains no 2SLGBTQI+ specific warnings for Guadeloupe.
- **Bottom line:** Guadeloupe is among the more legally and socially secure destinations in the Caribbean for 2SLGBTQI+ travellers. The full application of French law provides strong protections, and the resort environment is welcoming and inclusive.
