# Fort-de-France, Martinique (FDF) — Vacation Guide for Couples from Toronto

**Trip profile:** Two adults departing Toronto Pearson (YYZ), late April 2026, approximately 5 nights.
**Currency note:** Flights are priced in CAD. All on-the-ground costs in Martinique are in **EUR** — Martinique is a French overseas department (région d'outre-mer) and a full member of the European Union. The Euro is the sole official currency. USD and CAD are **not accepted**. Exchange CAD to EUR before departing Canada; credit cards are widely accepted at hotels, restaurants, and larger shops.

## Hotels

Martinique offers a genuinely diverse accommodation landscape reflecting its French identity — from international all-inclusive resorts clustered in the south to intimate plantation-house boutique hotels in the lush north, with a strong tradition of French-Caribbean hospitality throughout. The island is not dominated by the mass-market all-inclusive model the way Cuba or the Dominican Republic are; many of the best properties are independent or small-group hotels with individual character.

Prices below are in USD (hotel pricing in Martinique typically uses EUR or USD for international travellers; CAD is generally not quoted by properties). Apply the current EUR/USD/CAD exchange rate for your own budgeting. Hotel taxes and resort fees vary by property — confirm at booking.

## Dining

Martinique is one of the finest culinary destinations in the Caribbean — full stop. As a French territory with a living Creole culture, the island brings together classical French technique, locally caught seafood, tropical produce, and a uniquely Martinican spice tradition built around the colombo blend (similar to curry), scotch bonnet peppers, allspice, and fresh lime. The result is a cuisine that is simultaneously sophisticated and deeply rooted in place.

**The essential things to eat and drink in Martinique:**

- **Accras de morue** — salt cod fritters, crispy and light, served as a starter or apéritif snack at virtually every restaurant on the island. The benchmark by which Martinican kitchens are judged.
- **Colombo de poulet or cabri** — chicken or goat braised in the colombo spice blend (a blend of coriander, cumin, turmeric, mustard seed, and chili brought by Indian indentured workers in the 19th century — a cornerstone of Martinican flavour). Rich, aromatic, and unlike anything else in the Caribbean.
- **Boudin créole** — spiced blood sausage, a Martinican street food and market staple. Served as a starter or snack; the quality varies but the best versions are exceptional.
- **Blaffs** — fish or shellfish (typically snapper, sea bass, or langoustine) briefly poached in an aromatic court-bouillon of lime juice, chili, thyme, and allspice. One of Martinique's most elegant preparations — clean, bright, and deeply flavoured. A true dish of the island.
- **Matoutou** — a traditional Creole preparation of land crabs with rice, turmeric, and vegetables, historically tied to the Easter and spring period. Late April in Martinique is prime matoutou season — do not miss it if you see it on a menu.
- **Ti' Punch** — the aperitif of Martinique: white rhum agricole, a squeeze of fresh lime, and a small spoon of cane syrup (sirop de canne). Served before virtually every meal. The ratio of rum to lime to syrup is a deeply personal matter — locals mix their own. It is the proper introduction to any meal on the island.
- **Rhum agricole** — Martinique produces the only rum in the world with a French AOC (Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée) designation. Made from fresh sugarcane juice (rather than the molasses used in most Caribbean rums), it has a grassy, vegetal, complex character unlike any other rum. Aged versions (vieux rhum) rival fine Cognac in complexity. A distillery visit is a cultural experience, not merely a tourist activity.

One of the best dinner options in the south for couples who want a proper sit-down meal.

**7. La Plantation (Habitation Clément)**
- **Cuisine:** French-Creole; fine dining in a historic setting

**8. Marché de Fort-de-France (the central market)**
- **Cuisine:** Street food and market stalls; Creole snacks and produce

An essential half-morning in Fort-de-France.

## Transportation

### Airport to Hotel

Martinique Aimé Césaire International Airport (FDF) is located in **Le Lamentin**, approximately **6 km east of Fort-de-France** and about **20–30 minutes by road** from the Trois-Îlets/Pointe du Bout area (via ferry connection) or **1 hour or more** from Sainte-Anne in the south.

**Taxis:**
- Official taxis are available at the airport's taxi rank. Fares are regulated and fixed by zone:
  - FDF Airport to Fort-de-France city: approximately **EUR $25–$35**
  - FDF Airport to Les Trois-Îlets / Pointe du Bout area: approximately **EUR $35–$50**
  - FDF Airport to Sainte-Anne / Les Salines area: approximately **EUR $60–$90**
- All fares are in EUR. Confirm the fare before departure — taxis may surcharge for large luggage or late-night/Sunday service.
- Carry EUR cash as some drivers do not accept cards.

**Car rental:**
- Major international agencies (Europcar, Avis, Budget, Hertz, Sixt) have desks at FDF airport arrivals. **Picking up your rental car at the airport is the most efficient option** — especially if your hotel is outside Fort-de-France.
- See the Getting Around section below for full car rental details.

**Note:** There is no public bus or shuttle service from FDF airport to the main tourist areas. Rideshare apps do not meaningfully operate on the island. A taxi or rental car are your only practical options on arrival.

### Getting Around

**Car rental — strongly recommended:**
- Martinique has no useful tourist bus network. The island's sights, beaches, distilleries, and restaurants are spread across a landscape roughly 60 km north-to-south and 30 km east-to-west, connected by roads through mountains, rainforest, and coastal villages. Without a car, you are essentially confined to your hotel and whatever a taxi is willing to drive you to.
- **Renting a car is one of the most important decisions you will make for this trip.** It transforms Martinique from a passive beach holiday into an active exploration of one of the richest and most varied islands in the Caribbean.
- **Drive on the RIGHT** — Martinique is French territory and follows continental European road conventions. North American drivers will feel immediately at home, unlike British Caribbean territories.
- Rates: approximately **EUR $50–$90/day** for a compact or small SUV in late April. Book in advance, particularly during the Easter/spring holiday period when demand from French visitors is high.
- A valid Canadian driver's licence is accepted. Most agencies require a minimum age of 21; under-25 surcharges may apply.
- The island is small enough that you can drive from Fort-de-France to Sainte-Anne in about **1 hour**, or from the capital to Saint-Pierre in about **45 minutes**. Allow extra time on the winding mountain roads of the interior.
- **Fuel:** Petrol stations are widely available in towns. Fill up before heading into the north or interior — stations become sparse on mountain routes.

**Water taxis (vedettes) — Fort-de-France to Les Trois-Îlets:**
- A network of water taxis (vedettes) crosses the bay between Fort-de-France and Pointe du Bout / Les Trois-Îlets / Anse Mitan. The crossing takes **25 minutes** and is scenic, practical, and far faster than driving around the bay.
- Fare: approximately **EUR $8–$12/person** one way.
- Departures are frequent during the day (roughly every 30 minutes in season). Check the current schedule on arrival — a stay in Les Trois-Îlets combined with regular water-taxi trips to Fort-de-France is one of the most pleasant itinerary structures for this island.

**Taxis for day trips:**
- For couples without a rental car who want to do occasional excursions, taxis can be hired by the half-day or full day. Negotiate the rate in advance — full-day rates of EUR $150–$250 for a driver-guide are reasonable for island touring.

## Attractions

### 1. Les Salines Beach (Sainte-Anne)
- **Hours:** Always accessible
- **Admission:** Free
- **Description:** Consistently rated one of the finest beaches in the entire Caribbean, Les Salines is Martinique's showpiece. A long crescent of fine white sand fringed by coconut palms, with water that transitions from shallow turquoise to vivid deep blue. Calm, clear, and warm — sea temperature in late April is approximately **27°C (80°F)**. The beach is lined by natural shade from coconut palms rather than resort infrastructure; the only facilities are a small parking area and some snack vendors. It feels genuinely wild and natural while being easily accessible. Located at the very southern tip of the island, just past Sainte-Anne.
- **Tip:** Arrive early (before 10 AM) on weekends and public holidays when French families from across the island descend in numbers. Weekday mornings are blissfully quiet. Bring your own food and water — the snack options are limited and the beach is best enjoyed for several hours.

### 2. Mount Pelée (La Montagne Pelée)
- **Hours:** The summit trail is accessible year-round; depart by 7–8 AM for the best conditions
- **Admission:** Free (no entry fee for the park)
- **Description:** An active stratovolcano and the most dramatic natural landmark in the French Caribbean. Mount Pelée stands at 1,397 metres and is permanently shrouded in cloud forest above 800 metres — a world of towering tree ferns, bromeliads, orchids, and the incessant sound of rain and wildlife. The hike to the summit and back takes approximately **4–5 hours round trip** on a well-maintained trail from the parking area at l'Aileron. The views from the summit crater rim — when the clouds briefly part — are extraordinary: the entire island laid out below, Dominica to the north, Saint Lucia to the south.
- **Tip:** A local guide is strongly recommended for the summit hike — the weather changes rapidly, the crater rim is exposed, and a guide adds significant historical and ecological context. The mountain is an active volcano (last major eruption 1932; serious activity 1902) and conditions are monitored. Check current volcanic status before hiking; go with an authorised guide agency from Saint-Pierre or Le Prêcheur.

### 3. Saint-Pierre — the City of Fire
- **Hours:** Always accessible; diving operators depart from the harbour daily
- **Admission:** Musée Vulcanologique ~EUR $5/person; wreck diving ~EUR $60–$90/person per dive
- **Description:** On May 8, 1902, Mount Pelée erupted with catastrophic force, destroying the city of Saint-Pierre — then the most cosmopolitan city in the French Caribbean, known as the "Paris of the West Indies" — in under two minutes. Approximately 30,000 people died. Almost nothing survived. Today, Saint-Pierre is a small town built among and atop the ruins of the 1902 city. Ruined walls, stone facades, and the skeletal remains of the old theatre and cathedral rise from the vegetation. The Musée Vulcanologique (founded by the American vulcanologist Franck Perret, who lived through the aftermath) documents the disaster with remarkable photographic and artefactual records.
- Offshore, the 1902 eruption sank 18 ships in the harbour. These wrecks — ranging from small coastal schooners to large sailing ships — are now encrusted with coral and teeming with marine life. They are considered **world-class wreck dives** accessible to relatively inexperienced divers due to their moderate depth (15–50 metres). Even non-divers can snorkel over the shallower wrecks.
- **Tip:** Saint-Pierre is best visited as a full-day excursion combining the town ruins, the museum, lunch at a waterfront restaurant, and an afternoon dive or snorkel. Several dive operators are based in Saint-Pierre harbour.

### 4. Route de la Trace (N3)
- **Hours:** Always accessible by road
- **Admission:** Free
- **Description:** The Route de la Trace is the mountain highway that runs north-south through the rainforest interior of Martinique, passing through the Parc Naturel Régional de la Martinique. It is one of the most scenic drives in the entire Caribbean. The road winds through dense tropical rainforest with towering tree ferns, giant bamboo groves, waterfalls visible from the roadside, and hummingbirds (foul-kolibri in Creole) visiting flowering plants along the verges. Several hiking trailheads branch off the route into the forest interior.
- Key stops along the route include the **Jardin de Balata** (see below), the **Gorge de la Falaise** (a river canyon with a guided wade-and-swim excursion through the gorge), and numerous viewpoints over the Caribbean coast and Atlantic coast simultaneously.
- **Tip:** Drive the full route north to south (or reverse) and plan at least 3–4 hours to stop and explore. The road is well-maintained but narrow in places; an early start avoids the worst traffic around Fort-de-France.

### 5. Rhum Agricole Distillery Visits
- **Hours:** Vary by distillery; generally 9 AM–5 PM daily; guided tours run at set times
- **Admission:** Generally EUR $10–$20/person for a guided tour; tasting included
- **Description:** Martinique's AOC (Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée) rhum agricole is made from fresh sugarcane juice — harvested, pressed, and fermented within hours — rather than the molasses by-product used in most Caribbean rums. The result is a rum with a grassy, vegetal complexity and terroir-driven character that no other Caribbean rum can match. The AOC designation (the first and only rum AOC in the world) is genuine: production methods, geographic origin, and ageing standards are all regulated.
  - **Habitation Clément** (Le François): The island's most visited distillery estate; beautifully preserved plantation grounds, a rum museum with centuries of artefacts, a contemporary art gallery, and an excellent restaurant. The aged rhum vieux expressions are outstanding. Probably the best overall distillery experience for visitors.
  - **JM Distillery** (Macouba, far north): Smaller, more remote, and producing some of the most highly regarded rhum agricole on the island. The setting — tucked into the northern mountains near Pelée — is spectacular. Worth the drive.
  - **La Mauny** (Rivière-Pilote, south): A well-run distillery with good tours and a wide range of products; more accessible in terms of location from the south-coast hotel areas.
- **Tip:** Distillery tours run during crushing season (roughly February–July for most), which aligns with a late April visit. You may be able to see the cane being processed — a genuine bonus.

### 6. Les Anses d'Arlet
- **Hours:** Always accessible
- **Admission:** Free
- **Description:** A small fishing village on the south Caribbean coast where the bay is dotted with coloured fishing boats, the water is crystalline, and the village church — the Église Saint-Henri — sits directly on the waterfront, its cemetery extending to the sand's edge. The scene is one of the most photographed in Martinique and for good reason: it is genuinely beautiful and genuinely still a living fishing village, not a theatrical reconstruction. The snorkelling directly off the beach is excellent — sea turtles and a rich reef are accessible from the shore. Chez Gracieuse (see Dining) is steps from the water.
- **Tip:** The village is small enough that parking on a busy Sunday morning becomes competitive. Arrive early. Snorkel gear can be rented from a local shop on the beach.

### 7. Jardin de Balata
- **Hours:** Daily 9 AM–5 PM (last entry 4 PM)
- **Admission:** ~EUR $16/adult
- **Description:** A private botanical garden created by landscape architect Jean-Philippe Thoze on a hillside above Fort-de-France, along the Route de la Trace. The gardens contain thousands of tropical plant species — anthuriums, heliconia, orchids, bromeliads, towering palms, tree ferns — arranged with a French sense of design order amid the controlled chaos of the rainforest. The recent addition of a network of suspended footbridges through the rainforest canopy (treetop walkways at heights of 8–12 metres) makes it unmissable: the view from the bridges across the canopy toward the Caribbean coast is spectacular. Hummingbirds are constant companions throughout the garden.
- **Tip:** Combine with driving the full Route de la Trace north as a full-day itinerary. Arrive at opening (9 AM) to have the canopy bridges to yourselves before tour groups arrive mid-morning.

## Beaches

### Les Salines (Sainte-Anne)
Martinique's most celebrated beach and one of the finest in the Caribbean. The wide crescent of fine white sand is shaded by coconut palms, the water is clear and warm (approximately 27°C in late April), and the setting feels natural rather than resort-engineered. The southern tip of the island location means it catches gentle Caribbean swells but remains calm enough for comfortable swimming throughout the day. A short walk north of the main beach reveals the quieter and equally beautiful **Grande Anse des Salines** — same sand, slightly fewer visitors.

### Les Anses d'Arlet
The beach of the famous fishing village on the south Caribbean coast. The water here is exceptionally clear, with a sandy bottom and healthy reef visible from the surface. Sea turtles are regularly encountered while snorkelling directly from the beach. The setting — framed by the village church, coloured fishing boats, and the green hills above — makes this not only one of Martinique's best beaches for snorkelling but one of its most photogenic overall.

### Anse Noire and Anse Dufour (Les Trois-Îlets area)
Two dramatic black-sand volcanic beaches tucked between headlands south of Les Trois-Îlets, accessible on foot or by car on a winding coastal road. Anse Noire is the darker of the two — its sand is jet-black volcanic material, startling against the turquoise water. Anse Dufour, just minutes away, has slightly lighter grey sand and is used by local fishing boats. Both bays are sheltered, calm, and excellent for snorkelling. The contrast between the dark sand, the vivid water, and the tropical vegetation is unlike any white-sand beach on the island. Turtles are frequently spotted at Anse Dufour.

### Pointe Marin (Sainte-Anne)
A long, gently curving beach just north of Sainte-Anne village, with shallow, calm, brilliantly clear water — one of the safest swimming beaches on the island. The shallow gradient and minimal wave action make it the best beach for leisurely wading and paddling. Windsurfing and kiteboarding schools operate from Pointe Marin due to the consistent breeze. There is more infrastructure here (some beach clubs, restaurants, equipment rental) than at the wilder Les Salines.

### Anse Céron (Le Prêcheur, north)
A remote black-sand beach at the northwestern tip of the island, near the town of Le Prêcheur at the foot of Mount Pelée. The sand is dark grey-black; the water deep blue; the backdrop dominated by the forested flank of Pelée above. The road north from Saint-Pierre winds through banana plantations and rainforest to reach it. Few tourists make the effort — those who do have a wild, dramatic beach largely to themselves. A strong contrast to the manicured south-coast scene.

## Practical Info

### April Weather

Late April is an excellent time to visit Martinique. The island sits outside the main hurricane belt and enjoys warm, sunny conditions year-round. Late April falls between the dry season (December–May, known as *carême*) and the start of the rainy/hurricane season (June–November, known as *hivernage*). Expect predominantly sunny days with brief afternoon showers that do not significantly disrupt a beach or outdoor itinerary.

| Metric | Late April Average |
|---|---|
| Daytime high | 29°C (84°F) |
| Overnight low | 23°C (73°F) |
| Sea temperature | 27°C (80°F) |
| Sunshine | ~9 hours/day |
| Humidity | ~70–75% (warm and tropical; lower than June–October) |
| Rainfall | Brief afternoon showers common; rarely prolonged |
| UV Index | 12 — **Extreme** |

- Late April coincides with **Easter and the French spring school holidays** (vacances de Printemps), which typically overlap with mid-to-late April. This is one of the busiest periods of the year for Martinique as French families from metropolitan France and the French Caribbean all converge on the island simultaneously. Expect fuller beaches, busier restaurants, and higher hotel prices than January or February. Book everything well in advance.
- **UV warning:** A UV Index of 12 is the top of the extreme scale — unprotected skin can burn in under 10 minutes at midday. Apply SPF 50+ sunscreen liberally and reapply every 90 minutes, especially on the beach. The tropical sun here is significantly more intense than anything Canadians are accustomed to.
- Hurricane season officially begins June 1. Late April carries essentially no hurricane risk.

### Currency

The **Euro (EUR)** is the sole official currency of Martinique. This is non-negotiable: Martinique is a full French department (département d'outre-mer) and full EU member, using the Euro as its national currency. Canadian dollars and US dollars are **not accepted** in everyday transactions.

- **Exchange CAD to EUR before departing Canada.** Your bank, a currency exchange service, or the airport are your best options. The rate at your home bank is generally better than airport exchange counters.
- **Credit and debit cards** (Visa, Mastercard) are accepted at all hotels, most restaurants, and larger shops. Inform your bank before travel to avoid fraud holds. Chip-and-PIN is universal in France/EU — your Canadian chip-and-PIN card will work.
- **ATMs** (DAB — Distributeur Automatique de Billets) are widely available in Fort-de-France, major towns, and near tourist areas. They dispense EUR. Canadian debit cards on the Cirrus/Plus network generally work; check with your bank regarding international withdrawal fees (typically CAD $3–$8/transaction plus the exchange rate markup).
- Budget approximately **EUR $100–$200 per couple per day** for food, drinks, activities, and transportation, exclusive of accommodation. Martinique is not cheap by Caribbean standards — it is priced at French European levels for most goods and services.

### Electricity

- **Voltage:** 220V, 50 Hz — this is the standard French/European voltage and is **different from Canada's 120V, 60 Hz.**
- **Plugs:** Type E (French two-round-pin with a grounding hole). These are **incompatible with Canadian plugs.**
- **IMPORTANT:** Canadian visitors need **both a voltage converter/transformer AND a plug adapter** for Martinique. Simply bringing a plug adapter without voltage conversion is not sufficient for many Canadian devices — a 120V Canadian appliance (especially anything with a motor, heating element, or complex electronics) connected to 220V without a converter will likely be damaged immediately.
  - **Exception:** Most modern laptops, phone chargers, and camera battery chargers are "dual voltage" (100–240V, 50–60 Hz) — check the label on your power brick. If it states this range, you only need a plug adapter (Type E), not a converter.
  - **Hair dryers, curling irons, electric razors, CPAP machines, and portable speakers** are frequently single-voltage and require a full converter. The safest solution is to buy a dual-voltage travel hair dryer before leaving Canada.
- Purchase a Type E plug adapter (and converter if needed) before departing Canada; they are available at travel accessory shops, Canadian Tire, or online. Do not rely on finding them at the destination.

### Language

**French** is the official language of Martinique. **Martinican Creole** (Kréyol Matinikè) is widely spoken in everyday life — particularly in markets, among older residents, and in less touristed areas. English is **not widely spoken** outside of the main resort properties (Club Med, Cap Est) and some tourist-facing businesses in Fort-de-France and Les Trois-Îlets.

- Basic French is not just polite — it is genuinely practical. Ordering in a restaurant, asking for directions, navigating the market, and communicating with taxi drivers will all be easier with even rudimentary French.
- The French in Martinique is Metropolitan French (not a strong accent for most Canadians who studied French); Quebec francophones will be immediately comfortable. Anglophone Canadians with any French school background will manage.
- Download the Google Translate app with French offline language packs before departure. It will help in tight spots.
- **Key phrases:** *Bonjour* (hello/good morning), *s'il vous plaît* (please), *merci* (thank you), *l'addition, s'il vous plaît* (the bill, please), *un ti' punch, s'il vous plaît* (a ti' punch, please — the only phrase you will truly need).

### Canada Travel Advisory

As of early 2026, the Government of Canada rates Martinique at **"Exercise a high degree of caution"** — Level 2 on Canada's four-level advisory scale. The advisory notes crime concerns, particularly in the **Fort-de-France city centre and certain suburban areas**, where gang-related crime and property crime have elevated in recent years.

- In practice, the main tourist areas of Martinique — the beaches of the south, the coastal villages, Les Trois-Îlets, the hotel corridors — are generally safe for tourists and operate with the normalcy of a French department.
- **Fort-de-France city centre** warrants genuine awareness, particularly after dark and in less-frequented areas. Keep valuables secure, avoid isolated streets at night, and use the same judgment you would in any major city.
- **Check travel.gc.ca** for the current advisory immediately before departure — advisory levels can change.
- Travel medical insurance and comprehensive trip cancellation insurance are strongly recommended regardless of advisory level.

### Health Notes

- **No mandatory vaccinations** are required for entry to Martinique for Canadian citizens (Martinique is an EU territory with French health standards).
- **Routine vaccinations** should be current: COVID-19, influenza, MMR, tetanus/diphtheria.
- **Hepatitis A and B** vaccinations are recommended (standard travel precaution for any tropical destination).
- **Medical facilities:** Martinique has a full French-standard public hospital system. The **Centre Hospitalier Universitaire (CHU) de Martinique** in Fort-de-France is a full-service teaching hospital with emergency, surgical, and intensive care services. This is significantly better healthcare infrastructure than most Caribbean islands. For non-emergency care, French-standard clinics are available throughout the island.
- **Travel medical insurance is essential.** Canadian provincial health plans (OHIP etc.) provide no coverage outside Canada. A policy covering emergency medical treatment, hospitalization, and medical evacuation is strongly recommended. Note that in Martinique, you can generally receive treatment at the public hospital on a fee-for-service basis; private insurance will reimburse costs.
- **Sun:** The primary health risk for Canadians. Apply SPF 50+ liberally, reapply every 90 minutes, wear a broad-brimmed hat, and seek shade during peak midday hours (11 AM–2 PM). The UV index of 12 is at the extreme end of the scale.
- **Mosquitoes:** Present, and Martinique has had intermittent Dengue fever and Chikungunya activity in recent years. Use DEET-based repellent (30%+) especially at dusk and dawn, wear long sleeves in the evenings, and use air conditioning or mosquito nets when sleeping. Check Public Health Agency of Canada guidance before travel.
- **Water:** Tap water in Martinique meets EU/French standards and is safe to drink. Bottled water is widely available.
- **Sea urchins:** Common on rocky reef areas. Wear water shoes or reef boots when entering the water at rocky beaches (Anse Noire, Anse Dufour, etc.). Stepping on a black sea urchin is extremely painful and a common tourist injury.

### Tipping Customs

Martinique follows **French tipping conventions**, which differ significantly from North American norms. Tipping is not mandatory, not expected at the US/Canada level, and service (service compris) is legally included in restaurant bills throughout France and its overseas territories.

| Situation | Suggested Amount |
|---|---|
| Restaurant (sit-down) | Service is legally included (*service compris*) in all bills. A small rounding-up (EUR $2–$5 for a good meal for 2) is appreciated but optional. |
| Hotel housekeeper | EUR $2–$3/night if you wish to leave something; not expected |
| Hotel porter/bellhop | EUR $1–$2 per bag |
| Taxi driver | Rounding up the fare to the nearest EUR $5 is common; a tip beyond this is not expected |
| Tour guide / distillery guide | EUR $5–$10/person for an excellent guided experience |
| Dive guide / boat captain | EUR $10–$15/person for a full dive excursion |
| Bar staff | Not expected; rounding up is fine |

- **Verify your bill:** French law requires the service charge to be included in menu prices — you are not tipping on top of a service-excluded price. The amount you see on the menu is what you pay, inclusive of service.
- Budget approximately **EUR $50–$100 per couple** specifically for gratuities over a 5-night stay — significantly less than at US-convention Caribbean destinations.

### Entry Requirements for Canadians

- **Passport:** A valid Canadian passport is required for entry to Martinique. Passport should be valid for the duration of your stay; most travel advisors recommend at least 6 months validity beyond your return date as a general precaution.
- **Visa:** No visa is required for Canadian citizens for tourism stays of up to 90 days within the EU Schengen area. Martinique, as a French department, is part of the EU but is **outside the Schengen area** for immigration purposes. In practice, entry is treated identically to entering metropolitan France — no visa required, passport stamped on arrival.
- **Tourist card:** Not required.
- **No ETIAS required** (for now): The EU's new ETIAS travel authorization system, when fully implemented, will apply to visa-exempt travellers entering Schengen territory but it does not yet fully apply to Martinique's external EU status. Verify the current status at travel.gc.ca before departure as this may change.
- **Time zone:** Martinique observes **Atlantic Standard Time (AST) — UTC−4** year-round, with no daylight saving time. In late April, Toronto is on Eastern Daylight Time (EDT — UTC−4). **There is no time difference between Martinique and Toronto in late April.** No jet lag adjustment required.

### 2SLGBTQI+ Travellers

- **Legal status:** French law applies fully to Martinique. Same-sex marriage has been legal throughout France (including all overseas departments) since 2013. All French anti-discrimination laws protecting on the basis of sexual orientation and gender identity apply equally.
- **Resort and tourist areas:** The main tourist areas — the beach corridors of the south, Les Trois-Îlets, Sainte-Anne, the international resorts — are welcoming and professional to all couples. Club Med, Cap Est, and the larger hotel properties are well-accustomed to international visitors from diverse backgrounds.
- **Fort-de-France and the broader community:** Martinique is a Caribbean society with deep Catholic and Creole cultural roots; attitudes in rural communities and among older residents can be more conservative than in metropolitan France. Public displays of affection between same-sex couples may draw attention outside the resort and tourist zones. Most LGBTQ+ travellers report no incidents, and French legal norms mean overt hostility is rare, but social conservatism exists below the surface of the tourist experience.
- **Martinique Pride:** There is a small but growing LGBTQ+ visibility in Martinique, particularly among younger urban residents in Fort-de-France. The island is far more progressive than most English-speaking Caribbean destinations, where criminalization was historically the norm.
- The **Canada travel advisory** for Martinique contains no specific 2SLGBTQI+ safety warnings.
- **Bottom line:** For a couples' vacation centred on beaches, food, distilleries, and outdoor activities within the main tourist areas, Martinique is a safe and welcoming destination. The French legal framework provides strong protections. Social conservatism in rural/traditional contexts is a consideration for travellers planning significant time off the tourist track.
